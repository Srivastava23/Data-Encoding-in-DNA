% Abstract (do not insert blank lines)
\abstract{DNA offers a very dense medium for archival storage, but synthesis and sequencing introduce substitution, insertion, and deletion errors. This paper evaluates an encoding pipeline that combines a recurrent Proximal Policy Optimisation agent, which chooses a dibit-to-nucleotide mapping for each of four chained blocks of a sequence, a neural error predictor, and multi-copy consensus voting. Agents were trained at a single length of 128 nucleotides and evaluated under a simulated sequencing channel with seven seeds and five lengths from 64 to 1024 nucleotides, against a fixed mapping and random mappings. At 64 nucleotides, three-copy voting raised decode accuracy from 76.23 to 81.69 percent, and in a separate scaling experiment ten copies reached 88.96 percent against 76.31 percent for a single copy. The learned mappings did not differ significantly in error rate from the fixed mapping (smallest unadjusted \textit{p} = 0.081, none surviving Holm--Bonferroni correction) and stayed within one percentage point of random mappings in decode accuracy, although they produced more GC-balanced sequences. Redundancy therefore accounts for most of the gain in this setting. The study relies on simulation, includes no error-correcting-code baseline, and treats the agents' action indices as known to the decoder.}

\keyword{consensus voting; DNA data storage; encoding optimisation; predictive error modelling; reinforcement learning}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Global data production shows no sign of slowing down. The International Data Corporation projects that the global datasphere will grow from about 33~zettabytes in 2018 to about 175~zettabytes by 2025~\cite{reinsel2018}, a volume that strains the physical limits of magnetic and solid-state storage in both density and long-term durability. That mismatch has pushed researchers toward alternative archival substrates.

Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is one such substrate. It can pack roughly 215~petabytes of information into a single gram~\cite{erlich2017} and, when preserved appropriately, can remain chemically stable for thousands of years~\cite{grass2015}.

A short primer on the biology helps readers who are new to the area. DNA is a chain of nucleotides, and each nucleotide carries one of four bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), or thymine (T). For our purposes a strand is simply an ordered string over this four-letter alphabet. Storing digital data in DNA means translating binary data into bases (encoding), manufacturing the strand base by base (synthesis), reading it back with a sequencer (sequencing), and translating the readout into binary again (decoding). Neither synthesis nor sequencing is perfect. Bases are substituted for the wrong base, extra bases are inserted, and bases are dropped, and these errors motivate the error-correction techniques studied here. Reviews of DNA encoding techniques are available elsewhere~\cite{chandra2025encoding}.

Synthesis and sequencing together introduce substitutions, insertions, and deletions at combined per-nucleotide rates that typically fall between 1\% and 4\%~\cite{ross2013, heckel2019}. These errors are not spread uniformly. Long homopolymer runs, where the same nucleotide repeats consecutively, are hard to synthesise and prone to sequencing errors~\cite{erlich2017}, and the same is true of sequences whose GC~content strays outside the 40--60\% window~\cite{ross2013}.

Most encoding schemes sidestep this context dependence. They map binary data to nucleotides with one rule fixed in advance, typically assigning each two-bit pair (dibit) to one predetermined base. Fixed mappings are simple and deterministic, but they cannot adapt to the statistics of individual data blocks. A mapping cannot change how often a dibit repeats, because any one-to-one mapping turns a repeated dibit into a repeated base, so homopolymer runs inside a block are set by the data and not by the mapping. What a mapping does control is which base each dibit value receives, and therefore the GC~content of a block and the bases that meet at block boundaries. If a block is skewed toward dibit values that the default mapping sends to G and C, the encoded block will have extreme GC~content even when a different mapping would have balanced it.

This observation is the starting point of our work. We treat the choice of mapping as a sequential decision problem: a sequence is split into consecutive blocks, and a Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) agent with a recurrent policy chooses a dibit-to-nucleotide mapping for each block in turn, conditioned on the statistics of that block and on the choices made before it. A companion multilayer perceptron estimates the expected error rate of a candidate encoding, and a multi-copy redundancy layer adds error correction through majority voting across independently sequenced copies.

Prior DNA storage systems~\cite{church2012, goldman2013, erlich2017, organick2018} have shown that digital data can be encoded into and recovered from synthetic DNA. They fix the encoding rule in advance and handle errors outside the mapping, through redundancy, coding, or both, although some build constraints into the encoding itself, for example the rotating code of Goldman \textit{et al.}~\cite{goldman2013}, which avoids repeated bases, and the screening of candidate strands for GC~content and homopolymer runs in DNA Fountain~\cite{erlich2017}. To our knowledge none of these selects the mapping adaptively for each data block, and characterisations of the storage channel~\cite{heckel2019} document the error patterns that such designs try to avoid. Reinforcement learning has been effective for combinatorial and sequential decision problems in adjacent domains~\cite{sutton2018, mazyavkina2021, angermueller2020}, and in genomics it has been applied to motif discovery~\cite{wang2022rlmd} and, more broadly, to gene regulatory network control, sequence alignment, and genome assembly~\cite{karami2024genomics}. We are not aware of earlier work that applies it to the choice of encoding in DNA storage, and this paper examines whether it helps.

The paper makes three contributions. It formulates block-adaptive dibit-to-nucleotide mapping as a sequential decision problem with a recurrent policy, a 24-action space per block, and a predictive error model folded into the reward. It measures multi-copy consensus voting from one to ten copies across five sequence lengths, which is the largest and most consistent effect we observed. And it reports a multi-seed evaluation with a random-mapping control, evaluation at lengths other than the single training length, and reward ablations, in which the learned mappings did not significantly outperform fixed or random mappings under our simulated channel.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

Consider a binary input sequence $\mathbf{b} = (b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_{2n})$ that is to be stored in DNA. Encoding begins by partitioning $\mathbf{b}$ into $n$~dibits $(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$, each dibit $d_i = (b_{2i-1}, b_{2i})$ taking a value in $\{00, 01, 10, 11\}$. A mapping $\sigma: \{00, 01, 10, 11\} \rightarrow \{A, C, G, T\}$, a bijection between dibits and nucleotides, converts each dibit into a base. The alphabet admits $4! = 24$ such bijections.

In the multi-block formulation the dibit array is divided into $K = 4$ consecutive blocks $\mathbf{d}^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{d}^{(4)}$ of equal size, so the blocks contain 16, 32, 64, 128, and 256 nucleotides for sequences of 64, 128, 256, 512, and 1024 nucleotides respectively. The agent chooses an action index $a_k \in \{0, \ldots, 23\}$ for each block. Block $k$ is encoded with the mapping $\sigma_{m_k}$ whose index $m_k$ is obtained by block chaining:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    m_1 &= a_1, \\
    m_k &= \bigl(a_k + s_{k-1}\bigr) \bmod 24, \qquad k \geq 2,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:chaining}
\end{equation}
where $s_{k-1}$ is the sum of the bits of block $k-1$, taken modulo 24. The offset $s_{k-1}$ therefore depends on the data of the preceding block, and no separate header is stored for it. The DNA sequence is the concatenation $\mathbf{s} = \sigma_{m_1}(\mathbf{d}^{(1)}) \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, \sigma_{m_4}(\mathbf{d}^{(4)})$. Synthesis and sequencing subject $\mathbf{s}$ to a stochastic error channel (substitutions, insertions, and deletions), producing a corrupted observation $\tilde{\mathbf{s}}$. Decoding proceeds block by block. Once block $k-1$ has been decoded, its decoded bits give the offset $s_{k-1}$, which is added to the action index $a_k$ to recover $m_k$, and the inverse mapping is applied to block $k$. Decode accuracy is the percentage of bits of the decoded bitstream $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$ that match $\mathbf{b}$.

Two consequences of this design matter for the results. First, the decoder uses the action indices $a_k$ as known inputs. In our evaluation they are given to the decoder, and the cost of storing them is not included in the accuracy figures (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Second, the offset is computed from decoded bits, so a block that is decoded incorrectly usually changes the offset and causes all later blocks to be decoded with a wrong mapping. Errors can therefore propagate from one block to the following ones.

The question we ask is whether a learned policy, given only statistics of the current block and the context before it, can choose the sequence of mappings $(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_K)$ so that decode accuracy improves relative to a fixed default mapping. The motivation is twofold. Sequencing errors cluster rather than spread evenly, since regions with long homopolymer runs or extreme GC~content are disproportionately error-prone~\cite{erlich2017, ross2013}. A mapping cannot shorten a homopolymer run inside a block, because any bijection preserves repeated dibits, but it can move a block's GC~content toward the balanced range and can change which bases meet at block boundaries, so a mapping chosen for a block's dibit distribution might avoid some of these error-prone motifs. And although 24~mappings is a small search space, the relationship between a block's statistics and the resulting sequence quality has no obvious closed form, which is the kind of problem a learned policy is meant for. Multi-copy redundancy adds a complementary mechanism: storing several copies and reconstructing the original by majority vote corrects errors that strike individual copies at random, without the overhead of algebraic error-correcting codes.

\subsection{System Overview}

The pipeline works as follows. Input bits are segmented into dibits and divided into blocks. The RL agent walks through the blocks in order and selects a mapping for each, and the sequence is encoded accordingly. The result is stored as $N$~redundant copies, each subjected to independent sequencing noise, and at retrieval a position-wise majority vote across copies yields a consensus sequence that is decoded with the corresponding inverse mappings. During training, a simulated sequencing channel stands in for the physical synthesis, storage, and sequencing process, and a reward (Section~\ref{sec:reward}) steers the agent toward encodings that decode accurately and, depending on the variant, keep GC~content balanced or have a low predicted error. The pipeline has three functional layers, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:system_overview}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\safefig{\textwidth}{fig0_system_overview.png}
\caption{A PPO agent with a recurrent policy (Layer~1) selects a mapping for each block, guided by the predictive error model (Layer~2); consensus voting (Layer~3) reconstructs the sequence.\label{fig:system_overview}}
\end{figure}
\unskip

\textbf{Layer~1: RL Encoding Agent.} A PPO-based actor-critic network with a GRU core takes a compact state vector that summarises each block and selects one of 24 mappings for it.

\textbf{Layer~2: Predictive Error Model.} A separate multilayer perceptron looks at features of the DNA sequence that a given encoding would produce and estimates its expected error rate. That estimate feeds back into the reward of the predictive agent, so the agent factors predicted sequencing risk into each decision.

\textbf{Layer~3: Multi-Copy Redundancy.} The encoded sequence is replicated into $N$~independent copies, each of which undergoes its own error simulation (or, in a physical deployment, its own round of synthesis and sequencing). A position-wise majority vote then produces a consensus strand for decoding.

Because the pipeline starts from an undifferentiated binary stream, it is not tied to a file format. The same logic applies to text, images, or any data that can be serialised into bytes. A web demonstration of the pipeline accompanies this work; it illustrates the mechanism on single runs and is not part of the aggregate evaluation reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Framework}\label{sec:reward}

\subsubsection{Environment}

Each episode covers one sequence. The agent makes one decision per block, from the first block to the last, and the sequence is then passed through the simulated channel and decoded. At the start of every episode a binary data block is drawn according to the curriculum in Section~\ref{sec:curriculum}.

\subsubsection{State Representation}

The state presented to the agent at block $k$ is an eight-dimensional real-valued vector:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_k = \bigl[g_k,\; h_k,\; H_k,\; k/K,\; \rho,\; m_{k-1},\; u_1,\; u_2\bigr]
    \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
Here $g_k$ is the GC content of block $k$, $h_k$ the length of its longest homopolymer run, $H_k$ its entropy, $k/K$ the normalised block index, $\rho$ the sequence length ratio, $m_{k-1}$ the index of the mapping chosen for the previous block, and $u_1, u_2$ the two preceding nucleotides.

\subsubsection{Action Space and Policy}

At each block the agent chooses one of $|\mathcal{A}| = 24$ actions, one for each of the possible bijections between dibits and nucleotides. The policy network applies a linear projection to the state, passes the result through a Gated Recurrent Unit~\cite{cho2014gru} with 64 hidden units, and feeds the GRU output to separate linear actor and critic heads. The recurrent state carries information about earlier blocks of the same sequence, so the choice for block $k$ can depend on the choices before it. The actor head defines a categorical distribution over the 24 mappings and the critic head outputs a scalar value.

\subsubsection{Reward Functions}

Two reward formulations were used. The RL agent without the predictor, and the agents of the ablation study, receive a terminal reward once the whole sequence has been encoded, passed through the channel, and decoded:
\begin{equation}
    R = w_a r_{\text{acc}} + w_g r_{\text{gc}} - w_h p_{\text{homo}} - w_e e_{\text{rate}}
    \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\text{acc}}$ is the bit-level decode accuracy, $r_{\text{gc}}$ is a GC-balance bonus that is largest when GC~content lies in $[0.40, 0.60]$, $p_{\text{homo}}$ is the normalised length of the longest homopolymer run, and $e_{\text{rate}}$ is the observed positional error rate. The weights are $w_a = 2.0$, $w_g = 1.0$, $w_h = 0.5$, and $w_e = 1.0$. The ablations remove the GC bonus or the homopolymer penalty. Within a block every mapping preserves run lengths, so the homopolymer term can respond to the agent's choices only where a run crosses a block boundary.

The predictive agent of the main comparison instead receives a signal at every block and a terminal signal. For block $k$ the local reward is
\begin{equation}
    r_k^{\text{loc}} = 0.5\,\bigl(\hat{e}^{\text{base}}_k - \hat{e}_k\bigr),
    \label{eq:local}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{e}_k$ is the error predicted for the chosen encoding of block $k$ and $\hat{e}^{\text{base}}_k$ is the corresponding prediction for a baseline reference. At the end of the sequence the agent receives $1.0\,(A - A^{\text{base}})$, where $A$ is the decode accuracy of the sequence and $A^{\text{base}}$ is the baseline accuracy. Because the two agents differ in the structure of their reward as well as in the use of the predictor, their comparison is not a controlled test of the predictor alone, a point we return to in the Limitations.

\subsubsection{Policy Optimisation}

Training uses the clipped-surrogate PPO objective of Schulman \textit{et al.}~\cite{schulman2017}:
\begin{equation}
    L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \Bigl[
    \min\bigl(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\;
    \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),\, 1{-}\varepsilon,\, 1{+}\varepsilon)
    \hat{A}_t\bigr)\Bigr]
    \label{eq:ppo}
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t | s_t)$ is the probability ratio between the new and old policies, $\hat{A}_t$ is the estimated advantage, and $\varepsilon$ is the clipping parameter. Advantages are estimated with generalised advantage estimation~\cite{schulman2016gae} using a discount factor $\gamma = 0.99$ and $\lambda = 0.95$, since each sequence involves one decision per block. The total loss combines policy, value, and entropy terms:
\begin{equation}
    L(\theta) = -L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) + c_v L^{\text{VF}}(\theta) - c_e H[\pi_\theta]
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
The entropy coefficient $c_e$ is reduced linearly from 0.01 to 0.005 over training. The learning rate is $3 \times 10^{-4}$ and the batch size is 64.

\subsubsection{Curriculum Training}\label{sec:curriculum}

Training data are random binary strings drawn from three distribution profiles: balanced, high-GC, and low-GC. To keep the agent from settling too early on easy distributions, training follows a curriculum that starts with the balanced profile, moves to a mixture of profiles, and ends with all profiles. All agents were trained at a fixed sequence length of 128 nucleotides, so every training block contains exactly 32 nucleotides. Every other evaluated length is therefore outside the training length, which makes the evaluation at 64, 256, 512, and 1024 nucleotides a test of generalisation from a single training length.

\subsection{Predictive Error Model}

A separate multilayer perceptron regresses the expected sequencing error rate from features of the encoded sequence: its length, GC~content, entropy, longest homopolymer run, and features of the mapping used. Its output enters the predictive agent's reward through the per-block term in Equation~(\ref{eq:local}). Figure~\ref{fig:predictor_loss} shows the predictor's training loss over 500 training steps of one run. The loss is noisy, peaks near 0.05 early in training, and settles around 0.041, so the decline is modest. We return to what this implies in the Discussion.

\subsection{DNA Encoding Strategy}

\subsubsection{Dibit Mapping}

Binary data is split into dibits from $\{00, 01, 10, 11\}$ and each dibit is mapped to a nucleotide through a bijection $\sigma$. The baseline mapping, index~0, sends $00 \to A$, $01 \to C$, $10 \to G$, and $11 \to T$. Enumerating all permutations of the alphabet gives the $4! = 24$ bijections. The pipeline applies no post-processing (no GC~smoothing and no homopolymer breaking), which keeps every mapping one-to-one and reversible, as lossless decoding requires.

\subsubsection{Context-Dependent Error Simulation}

The simulated channel has base error rates of 2\% for substitutions, 1\% for insertions, and 1\% for deletions. Local context modulates these rates through a position-dependent multiplier:
\begin{equation}
    m_i = \begin{cases}
    1 + 0.3 \cdot (h_i - 2), & \text{if } h_i \geq 3 \\
    1, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:homo_mult}
\end{equation}
where $h_i$ is the homopolymer run length at position~$i$. A second factor applies when the local GC~content $g_i$, computed over a window of 10 bases, leaves the range 0.35 to 0.65:
\begin{equation}
    m_i \leftarrow m_i \cdot \bigl(1 + 2.0 \cdot |g_i - 0.5|\bigr),
    \quad \text{if } g_i < 0.35 \text{ or } g_i > 0.65
    \label{eq:gc_mult}
\end{equation}
Local error rates are capped at 0.5 for substitutions and 0.3 for insertions and deletions. The 0.40 to 0.60 range used in the reward is the target for the GC~content of an encoded block, whereas the channel acts on short local windows, and the two thresholds are separate settings.

\subsubsection{Consensus Voting}

Given $N$ independently corrupted copies $\tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{(1)}, \ldots, \tilde{\mathbf{s}}^{(N)}$, a position-wise majority vote reconstructs the consensus strand:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{s}_j = \arg\max_{c \in \{A,C,G,T\}}
    \sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\bigl[\tilde{s}_j^{(k)} = c\bigr]
    \label{eq:consensus}
\end{equation}
Positions are compared from the start of the copies up to their median length, and no multiple sequence alignment is applied. An insertion or deletion in one copy therefore shifts all later positions of that copy relative to the others, a weakness we return to in the Limitations. Ties are resolved in favour of the symbol that appears first in the order of the copies, so with two copies every disagreement is decided by the first copy.

\subsubsection{Decoding}

Decoding reverses the process: the consensus strand $\hat{\mathbf{s}}$ is split into blocks and decoded block by block with the chained mappings of Equation~(\ref{eq:chaining}), and the decoded dibits are concatenated into the bitstream $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$. The bitstream is treated only as a sequence of bits, which is why the same logic carries over to any source data type.

\subsection{Evaluated Methods}

Five encoding methods were compared:
(i) the \textbf{Fixed Baseline}, the canonical mapping ($00 \to A$, $01 \to C$, $10 \to G$, $11 \to T$) applied to the whole sequence;
(ii) \textbf{Random Mapping}, a mapping drawn uniformly at random for each sequence and applied to the whole sequence;
(iii) the \textbf{RL Agent}, the PPO agent trained without the error predictor, with the terminal reward of Equation~(\ref{eq:reward});
(iv) \textbf{Predictive RL}, the PPO agent trained with the predictor-based local and terminal rewards of Equation~(\ref{eq:local}); and
(v) \textbf{Predictive RL + Redundancy}, the predictive agent with three-copy consensus voting.

\subsection{Evaluation Data}

Each method was evaluated on 100 trials per seed and sequence length, which gives 700 trials per method at every length. For a given seed and length the test sequences are frozen and identical for all methods, and the noise generator is seeded identically before each method is evaluated, so differences between methods are not caused by differences in the data. The agents were trained only at 128 nucleotides, so every other evaluated length lies outside the training length. The redundancy-scaling experiment of Section~\ref{sec:scaling} was evaluated separately from the main comparison, so its single-copy and three-copy values differ slightly from those in the main tables even though the agents are the same.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Results are aggregated over all seven seeds, each per-seed mean being an average over 100 trials, and the reported spread is the standard deviation across the seven per-seed means (divisor $n$). Independent two-sample $t$-tests on the per-seed mean error rates compare the predictive agent with the fixed baseline, the predictive agent with the RL-only agent, and the RL-only agent with the fixed baseline, at all five lengths (15 tests in total). We report the $t$-statistic as the first method minus the second, so a negative value means the first method has the lower error rate, and we apply the Holm--Bonferroni correction across the 15 tests. The gain from redundancy is assessed with paired $t$-tests across seeds. Random mapping and the stability scores are compared descriptively, without formal tests. Differences and percentages quoted in the text are computed from the rounded values shown in the tables, so a reader can reproduce them directly.

\subsection{Error Rate Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:error_rate} reports the average positional error rate as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over the seven seeds. This rate counts the mismatching positions over the common length of the original and the retrieved sequence, adds the difference in their lengths, and divides by the longer length. The agents were trained at a single length of 128~nt, so the blocks seen in training contained 32~nt. \subsection{GC Content and Sequence Stability}

Figure~\ref{fig:gc_distribution} shows the GC~content of the encoded sequences. Every method is centred near 0.50, with per-seed, per-length means between 0.468 and 0.530 and method averages between 0.498 and 0.504, so the average GC~content did not separate the methods. This is expected, because the training and test data mix high-GC, low-GC, and balanced profiles whose deviations cancel in the mean.

The stability score of a sequence is
\begin{equation}
    S = \max\Bigl(0,\; 0.5\,\bigl(1 - 2\,|g - 0.5|\bigr) + 0.5\,\bigl(1 - h_{\max}/n\bigr)\Bigr)
    \label{eq:stability}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the GC~content of the sequence, $h_{\max}$ the length of its longest homopolymer run, and $n$ its length, and Table~\ref{tab:stability} reports its average over trials. Both learned agents score above the fixed baseline and above random mapping at every length, and they did so in all seven seeds at every length. The learned agents exceed random mapping by 0.026 to 0.036 and the fixed baseline by 0.061 to 0.073, depending on length. At 256~nt, for example, predictive RL reaches 0.956 against 0.927 for random mapping and 0.887 for the baseline. The RL agent and the predictive agent are nearly identical, and the redundancy method shares the predictive agent's encoding and therefore its score.

The source of the difference can be identified from the exported columns. The longest-homopolymer term of Equation~(\ref{eq:stability}) is the same for all methods (the exported homopolymer fractions differ by at most 0.0014 between methods for any seed and length), so the difference lies in the GC term. Rearranging Equation~(\ref{eq:stability}) with the averaged homopolymer fraction $\bar{h}$ gives the mean absolute deviation of the GC~content from 0.5 in individual sequences as $1 - \bar{S} - \bar{h}/2$. We report this implied deviation in the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:stability}, assuming that the zero floor of Equation~(\ref{eq:stability}) is never reached, which holds because every exported per-seed score is above 0.84. As a consistency check, the implied deviation is never smaller than the deviation of the mean GC~content in any of the 175 per-seed, per-length entries, as it must be. At 64~nt the implied deviation is 0.113 for the fixed mapping, 0.077 for random mapping, and 0.051 for predictive RL, and at 1024~nt it is 0.099, 0.062, and 0.027. The learned agents therefore choose mappings that bring the GC~content of each individual sequence much closer to 0.5, which is the quantity a mapping can change (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), even though the mean over trials is the same for all methods.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Redundancy Dominates the Gain}

Multi-copy consensus voting is the only intervention with a clear and consistent effect. Three copies add between 1.63 and 5.46 points over the single-copy predictive agent, and going from one to ten copies adds between 5.71 and 12.65 points in the scaling experiment. By comparison, the learned mappings changed single-copy accuracy by at most 0.89 points relative to the fixed baseline. The mechanism behind the redundancy gain is simple. Substitutions, insertions, and deletions are drawn independently for each copy, so an error in one copy is unlikely to occur at the same position in the others, and majority voting removes it. The gain shrinks as sequences get longer. A plausible reason is that insertions and deletions accumulate with length and shift all downstream positions, which position-wise voting without alignment cannot repair, but we did not test this directly. The poor showing of two copies is consistent with the same picture: with only two copies a disagreement at a position cannot be settled by a majority, and the voting routine resolves each such tie in favour of the first copy.

\subsection{Learned Mappings and the Predictive Error Model}

The learned agents were not distinguishable from the fixed baseline in error rate (Table~\ref{tab:pvalues}) and were close to random mapping in both error rate and decode accuracy, and the ablations showed no consistent effect of any reward term. The training reward showed no sustained upward trend (Figure~\ref{fig:training_reward}), which fits this picture. We see several possible explanations, none of which we have isolated. Given the channel described in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, a permutation of the four bases may simply be too coarse an action to change error behaviour much. Within a block a bijection leaves run lengths unchanged, so the channel's homopolymer dependence is out of the policy's reach except at block boundaries, and only the GC dependence can be influenced through the choice of mapping. The channel penalises GC~content only in local windows outside 0.35 to 0.65, so balancing the GC~content of a whole sequence may buy little in accuracy.

The chaining rule may also limit the agent's control. For blocks after the first, the mapping that is actually applied is the chosen index shifted by an offset that depends on the bits of the previous block (Equation~\ref{eq:chaining}), and the state of Equation~(\ref{eq:state}) does not contain that offset directly. Unless the policy can anticipate the offset, its choice for the later blocks is shifted by an amount it does not control. The 8-dimensional state may also carry too little context for the recurrent policy to infer deeper structural risks. Finally, the predictor's loss declined only modestly (Figure~\ref{fig:predictor_loss}), and the predictive agent differs from the RL agent in its reward structure as well as in its use of the predictor, so the near-identical results of the two agents in Tables~\ref{tab:error_rate}, \ref{tab:decode_acc}, and~\ref{tab:stability} do not allow a clean conclusion about the predictor. A comparison against an oracle that picks the best of the 24 mappings for each block, an ablation that removes the offset, and a check of the predictor against a constant-mean baseline would help separate these explanations.

The one place where the learned agents differ clearly from the alternatives is the stability score (Table~\ref{tab:stability}), and Section~\ref{sec:results} showed that this difference comes from the GC term. The agents reduce the deviation of individual sequences from balanced GC~content by more than half relative to the fixed mapping, at every length, so what the policy learned does reach the encoded sequence. That improvement did not carry through to error rate or decode accuracy in our channel. One possibility is that the channel's context penalties are too mild for better GC balance to pay off, or that the GC and homopolymer reward terms trade against accuracy. We did not test either explanation.

\subsection{Sequence-Length Dependence}

Every method loses accuracy as sequences grow, and the benefit of three-copy redundancy shrinks from 5.46 points at 64~nt to 1.63 points at 1024~nt. The single-copy methods are close at every length, within 0.89 points of each other at most. The same conclusion holds across lengths, including the training length of 128~nt and lengths far from it: mapping choice had little effect here, and redundancy had a clear one.

Two mechanisms may contribute to the fall in accuracy with length, and we did not separate them. The first is the one noted above: insertions and deletions shift positions. The second is error propagation through block chaining. Block size grows from 16 to 256 nucleotides over the lengths studied while the per-base error rate is fixed, so the chance that an early block contains an error grows with length, and such an error usually changes the offset of Equation~(\ref{eq:chaining}) and leads to wrong mappings for the following blocks. Decoding with the true offsets would show how much accuracy this mechanism costs.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The results should be read with the following limitations in mind. All evaluations use a simulation with fixed base rates (2\% substitution, 1\% insertion, 1\% deletion) and a heuristic context modulation, whereas real sequencing platforms have position-dependent, platform-specific error patterns that the model does not capture. Decode accuracy ranges from about 57\% to 89\% across our conditions and is only 57\% to 63\% at 1024~nt, not far above the 50\% expected from guessing random bits, so the numbers are useful for comparing methods under a harsh channel but do not describe a deployable storage system.

Several limits concern the encoding scheme. The decoder uses the agent's action indices as known inputs, and their storage cost is not included in our results. If they were stored explicitly, four indices would need at most $4\log_2 24 \approx 18.3$ bits per sequence, which is 14.3\% of the 128 payload bits at 64~nt and 0.9\% of the 2048 payload bits at 1024~nt. Block chaining makes later blocks depend on the decoded bits of earlier ones, so errors can propagate, and we did not measure this effect separately. The fixed and random baselines apply one mapping to the whole sequence, whereas the learned agents use four chained blocks, so differences between them combine the effect of mapping selection with the effect of chaining. The two learned agents also differ in reward structure, so their comparison does not isolate the predictor.

The consensus step is naive. Position-wise voting assumes that copies stay aligned, and insertions and deletions break that assumption as sequences get longer, which may help explain why the accuracy of even ten copies falls from 88.96\% at 64~nt to 63.04\% at 1024~nt, although we did not test this. A multiple sequence alignment step before voting could change the picture. Ties are resolved in favour of the first copy, so two copies give no majority benefit. Redundancy also carries a physical cost that raw accuracy hides: storing ten copies requires roughly ten times the synthesis effort, and algebraic codes such as Reed--Solomon or fountain codes, which are standard in practical pipelines~\cite{erlich2017}, can provide robustness with much less physical redundancy. We did not evaluate such codes, so the interaction between learned encodings and error-correcting codes is unknown.

Several limits concern the learned components. The action space of 24 mappings per block may be too restrictive to help, since within a block no mapping can alter run lengths, and the 8-dimensional state may carry too little context. The predictor's loss declined only modestly, so the benefit of an error-aware reward could not be tested properly, and the exported training curves cover only short windows of single runs, so we cannot show convergence behaviour over the full 100{,}000 epochs. The agents were trained at a single sequence length of 128~nt and we did not train on several lengths, so the results at other lengths describe generalisation from one training length. Finally, training and evaluation use synthetic binary data with controlled dibit distributions, seven seeds give limited power to detect small effects, and the ablation agents come from a single separate set of runs.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

Several directions follow from these findings. Measuring the accuracy of an oracle that chooses the best mapping per block would show how much headroom any mapping selector has, and decoding with the true offsets, or replacing the data-dependent offset by an explicitly protected mapping header and counting its overhead, would show how much accuracy block chaining costs. Training over several sequence lengths would show whether the null result depends on the single training length. A stronger predictor, checked against a constant-mean baseline and given more informative features, could make the error-aware reward meaningful. A richer action space, for example per-position decisions, might give a learned policy more to work with than a permutation of four bases. On the redundancy side, adding multiple sequence alignment before voting, a tie-breaking rule that uses read quality, or a learned reconstruction step such as the generative approach of Zheng \textit{et al.}~\cite{zheng2024dnagan}, is a natural extension, as is studying how consensus voting and learned encodings interact with Reed--Solomon or fountain codes. Validation against error profiles from Illumina, Oxford Nanopore, or PacBio would show whether the redundancy gains hold outside simulation, and splitting long streams into short addressed blocks of 64 to 128~nt, as in practical systems~\cite{organick2018}, would let the method scale while keeping the accuracy seen at short lengths.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We evaluated an adaptive DNA encoding pipeline built from a recurrent PPO agent that selects a mapping for each block, a predictive error model, and multi-copy consensus voting, using seven seeds and five sequence lengths from 64 to 1024~nt under a simulated sequencing channel, with the agents trained at a single length of 128~nt.

Three findings stand out. First, consensus voting gave the largest and most consistent gain. Three copies raised accuracy at 64~nt from 76.23\% to 81.69\%, and in the scaling experiment ten copies reached 88.96\% against 76.31\% for a single copy, a gain of 12.65 points, with smaller but consistent gains at longer lengths. Second, the learned mappings, with or without the error predictor, showed no significant difference in error rate from the fixed canonical mapping and performed similarly to random mappings, including at the training length, and removing individual reward components did not change results consistently. Third, the learned agents did give higher stability scores than both the canonical and the random mapping at every length, because they bring the GC~content of individual sequences closer to balance, so the policy changes the encoded sequence but not, in our channel, decode accuracy.

These conclusions apply to a simulated channel, to redundancy compared only with the absence of an error-correcting code, and to agents whose action indices are treated as known to the decoder, with no storage cost counted for them. The practical lesson from this study is that, under our channel model, redundancy matters far more than the choice of mapping, and that a multi-seed protocol with a random-mapping control and evaluation at several sequence lengths is needed before attributing gains to a learned encoder. Whether a better predictor, a richer action space, or alignment-aware consensus would change that conclusion, and whether the redundancy gains hold under real sequencing errors, are the main open questions.
