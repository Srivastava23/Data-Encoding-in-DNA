DNA is nature's most ancient and most durable information storage medium. A single gram of synthetic DNA can theoretically encode 215 petabytes of digital data, far exceeding the density of any existing magnetic or optical storage technology. Unlike hard drives that degrade within decades, DNA preserved in cool, dry conditions can remain intact for thousands of years, as demonstrated by the successful sequencing of woolly mammoth genomes from specimens frozen over 10,000 years ago.

The process of storing digital data in DNA involves four fundamental steps. First, the binary data (sequences of 0s and 1s) from a computer file is mapped to the four nucleotide bases that comprise DNA: Adenine (A), Cytosine (C), Guanine (G), and Thymine (T). Second, the resulting DNA sequence is chemically synthesised into physical DNA strands using automated oligonucleotide synthesis machines. Third, the synthesised DNA is stored — potentially for centuries — in controlled conditions. Fourth, when the data needs to be retrieved, the DNA is sequenced using technologies such as Illumina or Oxford Nanopore sequencing, and the nucleotide sequence is converted back to binary data.

However, this process is not error-free. During both synthesis and sequencing, three types of errors commonly occur: substitutions (where one nucleotide is incorrectly read as another), insertions (where extra nucleotides appear in the sequence), and deletions (where nucleotides are lost). These errors are not random — they are strongly influenced by the local sequence context. Regions with long runs of the same nucleotide (homopolymer runs, such as AAAA or GGGG) are particularly error-prone, as sequencing machines struggle to accurately determine the exact number of repeated bases. Similarly, regions with extreme GC content (the proportion of Guanine and Cytosine bases) experience higher error rates due to secondary structure formation during synthesis.

This research addresses the challenge of minimising these errors through intelligent encoding. Rather than using a fixed mapping from binary data to DNA bases, we employ a Reinforcement Learning agent that learns to select the optimal encoding mapping for each data block based on its statistical properties. By choosing mappings that naturally produce DNA sequences with balanced GC content and minimal homopolymer runs, the agent reduces the probability of sequencing errors before they occur. A companion error predictor network further enhances this process by estimating the expected error rate for each encoding choice, allowing the agent to factor predicted risk into its decisions.
